Why Supersonic Flight Failed — and What It Will Take to Succeed

May 4, 2025 Intro to Flight — Final Essay University of South Florida

I have always been interested in supersonic flight and how to make travel easier for everybody. Taking advantage of the last paper from the class Intro to Flight, I conducted a literature review on the Concorde, exploring challenges that need to be overcome to make supersonic flight feasible again. Below is the paper.

Introduction

26 years ago, one could fly from New York City to London in three and a half hours with the Concorde. Now, after more than half a century of development, the fastest one can fly the same route takes approximately 8 hours. This setback might sound counterintuitive — we had the technology decades ago — but it reflects an important factor: the market was not ready for supersonic flight yet, or the Concorde was not built for the global conditions of its time. Is the world ready for the next era of supersonic flight? What conditions must be met for supersonic or hypersonic aviation to succeed? Through discussing the challenges of faster-than-sound aircraft, the fall of the Concorde, and the socio-economic factors involved, this paper will attempt to answer those questions.

A Brief History of Faster-Than-Sound Flight

Supersonic flight was first achieved on October 14, 1947, when Captain Chuck Yeager broke the sound barrier in the Bell X-1 — a milestone born out of intense international competition fueled by war, political ambition, and technological rivalry. Within a year, British engineers pursued similar advances with the Miles M.52, and in the decades that followed, experimental programs continued to push speed boundaries, with aircraft such as the NASA X-43 reaching speeds approaching Mach 10. Today, supersonic capability is standard in military aviation, as modern fighter jets like the F-22 Raptor, Sukhoi Su-30, and Dassault Rafale all exceed the speed of sound. Given this steady progression, it would seem reasonable to expect commercial aviation to follow the same trajectory; however, no modern commercial airliners operate at supersonic speeds — raising the question: why?

Fundamentals of Supersonic Flow

Let's begin with different flow regimes. When an aircraft flies at speeds below about 80% of the speed of sound, or Mach 0.8, it is considered subsonic. From Mach 0.8 to 1.2, the aircraft operates in the transonic regime, and from Mach 1.2 up to Mach 5, it is classified as supersonic. This distinction is important for understanding both the aerodynamic behavior of the aircraft and the efficiency of flight.

When an object travels subsonic, it generates pressure (sound) waves that propagate in all directions. As speed increases, these waves begin to compress in front of the object. Once the object reaches the speed of sound, it effectively "keeps up" with the waves it generates. Beyond the speed of sound, the aircraft moves faster than the pressure disturbances it creates, preventing them from propagating ahead. Instead, they accumulate along a boundary known as the Mach cone. This cone represents the envelope of all pressure waves emitted by the aircraft, and its geometry is defined by the relationship between the speed of sound and the aircraft's velocity, where sin μ = 1/M.

An aircraft can fly efficiently when the four fundamental forces are balanced: lift, weight, drag, and thrust. Using wings, the aircraft converts thrust into lift, allowing these forces to remain in equilibrium during steady flight.

In the subsonic flow regime, lift is primarily generated by the pressure difference between the upper and lower surfaces of the wing. However, once the aircraft passes the speed of sound, shock waves begin to form, distorting the pressure field around the wing. This disrupts the pressure distribution that produces lift and typically reduces the lift coefficient.

For subsonic aircraft, drag is mainly composed of pressure drag (from the pressure difference between the leading and trailing edges) and skin friction. Once shock waves appear, however, an additional component — wave drag — is introduced. This results from the energy lost in forming and sustaining shock waves. The combination of reduced lift and increased drag makes supersonic flight significantly less efficient.

Per British Airways, the Concorde burned 5,638 gallons of fuel per hour while having a maximum capacity of 128 seats, whereas a Boeing 777 in the same era burned about 2,000 gallons per hour while carrying more than 300 passengers.

Aerodynamic Shape

To tackle the distinct flow regimes encountered in supersonic flight, extensive research has been carried out to identify suitable wing geometries. A large portion of supersonic aircraft, both military and commercial, utilize a configuration known as the delta wing — a triangular-shaped wing resembling the Greek letter delta.

Delta wings offer several strong advantages, especially in high-speed flight regimes. Their geometry is highly effective in transonic and supersonic conditions, where leading-edge vortices form and energize airflow over the upper surface, significantly increasing lift and delaying stall even at very high angles of attack. This makes them particularly stable and efficient at high Mach numbers.

However, these benefits come with clear drawbacks. At low speeds, delta wings are inefficient and require a high angle of attack to generate sufficient lift, which increases drag and makes takeoff and landing more demanding. In other words, flying supersonic comes at a cost: reduced efficiency during takeoff and landing.

Engine

Flying across different speed regimes also requires engines to be redesigned. While supersonic engines are optimized for high-speed cruise to reduce travel time, their most demanding performance conditions often occur at high subsonic speeds and during top-of-climb rather than at takeoff.

A particularly important area in supersonic propulsion is air intake design. Air intakes are responsible for delivering the correct airflow to the engine across all flight conditions — takeoff, cruise, landing, and everything in between. As the aircraft approaches supersonic speeds, the behavior of the airflow changes significantly, requiring specially designed inlets.

Designers must ensure stable operation and avoid phenomena such as buzz or unstart, where shock waves move out of the intake and disrupt airflow, potentially leading to engine flameout. Additional constraints include minimizing flow distortion at the engine inlet and maintaining a consistent mass flow rate.

Beyond aerodynamics and stability, engine intake design must also account for practical engineering factors such as weight, volume, integration with the aircraft structure, thermal and structural loads, variable-geometry mechanisms, bleed air systems, and even stealth considerations.

Heating and Structural Challenges

A significant challenge for supersonic and hypersonic flight vehicles is intense aerodynamic heating of the airframe, resulting from both air friction and convective heat transfer. This thermal environment posed a major design constraint for the Concorde.

Convective heating refers to the transfer of thermal energy from the air to the vehicle surface, where the kinetic energy of the incoming flow is partially converted into heat as the air decelerates and compresses within the boundary layer. In this region, temperature gradients develop between the hotter, compressed air near the surface and the cooler structure beneath, resulting in a continuous heat influx into the airframe.

The goal of aircraft design is to maximize key performance metrics while simultaneously satisfying operational and physical constraints. To address these heating limitations, new materials had to be developed for supersonic flight. For the Concorde, Hiduminium RR58 — a lightweight alloy with significantly greater thermal resistance than conventional aluminum — was extensively used.

In the past 20 years, there has been a wide body of research addressing all three major constraints: aerodynamics, engines, and materials/structural heating. The Concorde was even built and successfully flew while balancing all of these constraints. Yet there is another major setback that makes supersonic flight undesirable.

Sonic Boom

As we have seen above, once the vehicle reaches supersonic speed, pressure waves form a cone behind the aircraft. These waves then reach the ground and create a loud noise known as a sonic boom. A sonic boom can reach up to around 135 dB — enough to shatter glass — and this makes supersonic flight over cities or even entire continents impractical.

Several attempts have been made to mitigate sonic boom. These include Boom Supersonic's approach of managing and reducing the perceived boom through aircraft design and flight optimization, as well as NASA's Quesst mission, which focuses on shaping the aircraft geometry to carefully distribute pressure waves and reduce the intensity of the boom. However, no approach has yet fully solved the problem for widespread commercial use.

The Success of Concorde

Despite the enormous challenges supersonic flight must overcome — stringent aerodynamic design constraints, poor fuel efficiency, and environmental and regulatory concerns such as sonic booms — the Concorde still managed to operate for 27 years, from 1976 to 2003. But was that a true success, or an outlier in aviation history, given that no major commercial supersonic transport has been developed since?

One could argue it was more of an engineering and political achievement than an economic one. The Concorde was developed during the Cold War era, when technological supremacy was a key form of geopolitical competition. Several nations — including the United States, the Soviet Union, West Germany (which withdrew early), and the British–French partnership — pursued supersonic transport programs, but only the Concorde reached commercial service.

In total, only 20 Concorde aircraft were built, and just 14 entered passenger service, operated exclusively by British Airways and Air France, both with significant government support. No other commercial airlines ever operated the aircraft. Ticket prices were extremely high (roughly equivalent to $18,000–$20,000 in today's dollars), limiting demand to wealthy passengers and business travelers.

The Concorde primarily flew transatlantic routes, but its operation was constrained by high fuel consumption, extremely high maintenance costs, limited airport compatibility, strict noise regulations, and complex air traffic control procedures due to its supersonic speed. These factors ultimately made it economically unsustainable, despite its technological brilliance.

Discussion

What are the conditions that must be right for supersonic flight? Several aircraft concepts, engine concepts, and planned routes are already being researched and developed, but one can only speculate who will succeed. Nevertheless, there are some general conditions that are more likely to hold true.

First, supersonic flight is appealing because of its time-saving advantage, but this comes with very high fares that can only be justified by business travelers. A steady stream of business travel demand is therefore a primary requirement.

Second, supersonic flights were historically limited to transatlantic routes, because the Concorde's range was too short for many transpacific routes, and its sonic boom made overland transcontinental flight impractical. To make supersonic travel broadly viable, more routes would need to be added between major global cities such as Tokyo–New York or Hong Kong–New York.

Third, the aircraft must comply with existing aviation standards, including ATC procedures, takeoff and landing requirements, airport gate compatibility, and the ability to operate on both current jet fuel and future sustainable aviation fuels (SAF). This leaves limited design flexibility. The aircraft must be quiet enough to fly over land, fast and efficient enough to justify its cost, and compatible enough with existing infrastructure while still adaptable to future changes in aviation technology.

It is estimated that a global fleet of roughly 500–1,000 supersonic aircraft may be sufficient to serve the market, which would not leave much room for large-scale competition. Whoever successfully develops the next generation of supersonic aircraft could potentially dominate the entire market.

To summarize, supersonic flight is a regime that humankind has only stepped halfway into and has yet to fully commit to. For supersonic flights to become viable again, aircraft designers must understand not only the flow regimes in which the aircraft operates, but also the critical transition from subsonic to supersonic conditions. They must design a geometry suitable for that regime while remaining quiet enough for overland flight, develop engines that are efficient across all speed regimes, and build aircraft that are inexpensive enough to operate profitably — while still relying on a sufficient base of business travelers.

However, the demand landscape itself is also uncertain. Since COVID, there has been a noticeable shift toward more internet-connected business practices and fewer routine business flights, potentially reducing the core market that supersonic travel depends on.

Finally, developers must bring these aircraft to market quickly enough to establish dominance before competitors. These are no small feats. Still, the outlook remains bright and fascinating to watch — and potentially to be part of.

It would be incomplete not to mention the innovators currently working to achieve supersonic flight. Boom Supersonic, in 2025, achieved two supersonic flights with its XB-1 test aircraft, developed its own supersonic engine, and is exploring ways to reduce shock waves through aerodynamic shaping and atmospheric effects. Hermeus is focused on developing a turbine-based combined-cycle engine, Chimera. At low speeds, Chimera operates in turbine mode — like a conventional jet engine — while at higher speeds, it bypasses the turbine and transitions to ramjet operation. This approach improves efficiency by effectively combining two propulsion systems into one: a turbine for low speeds and a ramjet for high speeds.

Venus Aerospace is attempting a similar goal, aiming to develop a highly efficient, high-speed (hypersonic) engine. Astro Mechanica is pursuing a different path, working on a novel jet engine architecture that separates the turbofan from the turbogenerator. In this design, the turbogenerator drives an electric generator, which powers electric motors that independently drive the compressor and turbofan. This allows the engine to vary the amount of air being compressed and therefore control the volume of compressed air available for combustion with greater flexibility. Spike Aerospace is also developing a supersonic business jet, though with relatively limited public information, but its outlook remains promising.

In the end, whether supersonic flight returns on a scale will come down to a simple but unforgiving equation: can these technologies align with real-world economics and regulation at the same time. History has already shown — through Concorde — that technical success alone is not enough. The next generation must be quieter, cheaper, cleaner, and more adaptable to a changing travel landscape, all while delivering a clear advantage over increasingly efficient subsonic alternatives. What makes this moment different is not just the ambition, but the diversity of approaches being explored in parallel, from propulsion breakthroughs to aerodynamic refinement and novel business models. If even a few of these bets pay off, supersonic travel may finally move from a niche experiment to a sustainable industry. And if it does, it will not just mark the return of high-speed flight, but the beginning of a more mature and fully realized supersonic era.
