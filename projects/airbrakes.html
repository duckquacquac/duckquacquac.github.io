Airbrakes System Design

2025–2026 USF SOAR — IREC SolidWorks · ANSYS Fluent · MATLAB/Simulink

Airbrakes fully assembled at USF Engineering Expo.

SOAR is competing in the International Rocket Engineering Competition, where 143 teams compete to launch rockets in Midland, Texas. The team is scored based on several factors, including rocket build quality, payload challenge, and project report. Most importantly, 350 of 1,000 possible points are based on altitude achieved relative to target altitude. The formula for altitude points is:

$$\text{Points} = 350 - \left(\frac{350}{0.3 \times \text{Apogee}_\text{target}}\right) \times |\text{Apogee}_\text{target} - \text{Apogee}_\text{actual}|$$

Because of this high point component, the airbrakes sub-system is the team's ambitious attempt to get as close to target altitude as possible. The underlying assumption is that overshooting with the motor and relying on a control system is more reliable than predicting apogee from amateur high-power solid motor thrust data alone. This assumption is backed by the team's experience with deviations from manufacturers' provided thrust curves.

Final deliverable: a fully functional airbrakes system inside the rocket's midsection.

Governing Equation

To understand how the rocket behaves, we derive a model using Newton's second law. Assume the rocket is flying vertically, under no wind load, after motor burnout (coast phase). The rocket is under the effect of two forces only: drag and gravity. Strictly speaking, a lift component exists, but it cancels by symmetry.

The rocket is initially at $v_\text{cutoff}$ and $h_\text{cutoff}$, and $t = 0$ is set to the moment of motor cutoff. The positive direction is upward.

$$\sum F = ma$$ $$-mg - F_\text{drag} = ma$$ $$-g - \frac{F_\text{drag}}{m} = a$$

Integrating:

$$\int \left(-g - \frac{F_\text{drag}}{m}\right) dt = v + C$$ $$-gt - \int \frac{F_\text{drag}}{m}\, dt = v(t) + C$$

Since $v(0) = v_\text{cutoff}$:

$$v(t) = v_\text{cutoff} - gt - \int_0^t \frac{F_\text{drag}}{m}\, dt \tag{1}$$

Similarly, integrating $v(t)$ with respect to time gives altitude as a function of time:

$$h(t) = h_\text{cutoff} + v_\text{cutoff}\, t - \frac{1}{2}gt^2 - \int_0^t \left(\int_0^t \frac{F_\text{drag}}{m}\, dt\right) dt \tag{2}$$

Solving equations (1) and (2) gives the apogee of the rocket.

Simulink

Obviously, no one wants to solve this system of equations by hand. We developed a Simulink 3-DOF model to automatically solve for apogee.

The model implements a coast-phase flight dynamics simulation for a sounding rocket using MATLAB/Simulink. The primary signal chain is:

  • altToTemp — converts altitude to atmospheric temperature (K)
  • velocityToMach — computes Mach number from velocity and temperature (K)
  • dragModel — computes drag force ($F_d$) as a function of Mach number and flap angle, derived from CFD data
  • 3DOF (Body Axes), Fixed Mass — integrates equations of motion in the pitch plane

The model is structured for coast-phase analysis only, with no thrust input and fixed mass. This makes it appropriate for post-burnout apogee prediction and airbrakes control development, provided initial conditions are set to the burnout state.

During testing, a 10× increase in rocket mass produced an approximately 1,000 m increase in predicted apogee. This appears counterintuitive given that a heavier rocket also weighs more, but it has a physically valid explanation — with a critical caveat related to this model's decoupled structure.

Physics of Coast Phase Deceleration

During coast, the net deceleration is:

$$a = -\frac{F_\text{drag}}{m} - g$$

Increasing mass reduces the drag deceleration term $F_\text{drag}/m$, which dominates over the constant gravitational term $g$. The net result is a higher predicted apogee.

Simulink result — coast-phase flight dynamics model.

The model shows that with no flaps deployed, the apogee is approximately 3,500 m, which aligns closely with OpenRocket simulation.

Mechanical Design

After the concept generation phase, the final airbrakes geometry is four curved flaps opening radially outward, driven by a crank-slider mechanism.

Airbrakes CAD assembly.

Airbrakes skeleton diagram for kinematics and dynamics calculations.

The mechanical design maximises flap extension to increase drag. This prompted us to design the PCB with a central hole to save space for the lead screw. Everything else is compacted to the perimeter so the lead screw travel can be as long as possible.

The skeleton frame to hold the airbrakes is a design by Cesar, allowing easy integration into the rocket body and providing a common datum for all sub-systems to build upon.

Lead Screw Calculation and Motor Choice

To balance motor torque and actuation speed, a preliminary calculation was carried out using machine design principles. The governing equations are:

  • Torque to raise a load:

    $$T_{\text{raise}} = \frac{F \cdot D_m}{2} \cdot \left(\frac{L + u \cdot \pi D_m}{\pi D_m - u \cdot L}\right)$$

  • Torque to lower a load:

    $$T_{\text{lower}} = \frac{F \cdot D_m}{2} \cdot \left(\frac{L - u \cdot \pi D_m}{\pi D_m + u \cdot L}\right)$$

An Excel trade study was carried out comparing different lead screw configurations and motor specifications from McMaster-Carr, with maximum output force as the primary output metric. After comparing against expected aerodynamic loads, a NEMA 23 stepper motor was selected, paired with a ¼"-12 fast-travel lead screw.

CFD

Based on dimensional analysis, the drag force takes the form $F_d = f(\text{Ma}, \text{Re}, \text{Geometry})$. CFD models of the rocket at different flap angles were carried out, sweeping from 0 to Mach 0.8, with Reynolds number calculated directly from the launch profile to limit the parameter space. A total of 55 simulations were run across 6 geometry configurations (different flap angles).

CFD pressure contour map of the rocket with airbrakes deployed. Drag force is calculated from this.

The results show that drag force depends heavily on flap angle and Mach number, and is relatively insensitive to Reynolds number. Multiple models were fitted to the data; a second-order polynomial produced the best fit. This model was passed to the EECS team to program the flight controller.

CFD results — drag force vs. Mach number at varying flap angles, with polynomial fit.

Launch

After four days of delay, Aurora — USF's rocket — finally launched at IREC 2026, reaching an apogee of 9,035 ft. Due to a tilted rail guide and a lower off-the-rail speed than anticipated, the rocket undershot the target altitude and the airbrakes did not deploy. This is a regrettable outcome given a year of work, but I remain positive about future testing and the airbrakes' capability.
